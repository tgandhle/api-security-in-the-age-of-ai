Agent authority and approvals

Last reviewed 2026-10-01 · Jump to reference

Objective. After this module you can bind an approval to the exact parameters that will execute, say which fields an approval covers and which it merely accompanies, keep the summary a human read and the values a service used in agreement, and measure what a blanket approval actually authorises.

Before you start. Module 22 ended by saying that an injection which talks an agent into an allowed call with allowed arguments passes every check in that module. Human approval is the usual answer to that, and this module is about the gap between having an approval step and that step meaning something.

1. The attack

Running example: an ExampleAir support agent that can issue refunds. Company names are fictional. A human approves each refund above a threshold, in a queue, by reading a line that says what is about to happen and clicking approve.

The attack is not against the human. It is against the distance between the click and the execution. A proposal is written, read by a person, and then read again by the service that carries it out, and those are two different reads of a mutable thing.

OWASP's name for the underlying condition is LLM06:2025 Excessive Agency, defined as "the vulnerability that enables damaging actions to be performed in response to unexpected, ambiguous or manipulated outputs from an LLM, regardless of what is causing the LLM to malfunction". It names three root causes, and the third is the one this module addresses: excessive autonomy, meaning systems that lack independent verification and approval for high-impact actions. Its mitigation is to "Utilise human-in-the-loop control to require a human to approve high-impact actions".

The OWASP agentic list adds the complementary failure, ASI09 Human-Agent Trust Exploitation, in one sentence: "Confident, polished explanations misled human operators into approving harmful actions." Both of these are real. This module is about the half you can test.

2. Why it works

Two reads of a mutable thing

The structure of almost every approval queue is: the agent writes a proposal, a human reads it and approves, and a service reads it again and executes. Between the second and third step there is a window, and in that window the proposal is a row in a database that the agent, or anything that has talked the agent into something, can still write to.

Checks 1 to 4 of the lab are that window. The same approval, the same proposal id, a rewrite in between, and a refund of 4,000 to a different account where the human read "Refund 40 to T. Okafor". Nothing about the approval was forged. The approval was for a proposal id, and the proposal id still exists.

The fix is the same one this course has used since module 3: do not approve a reference, approve the content. The approval carries a MAC over the parameters as they stood when the human saw them, and the executor recomputes it over the parameters it is about to use. Check 5 is that same rewrite against a bound approval, and it names the two fields that changed.

An approval covers what it covers

This is module 4's lesson in a different costume. RFC 9421 calls it insufficient coverage, in section 7.2.1, and the principle does not care whether the thing signing is a client or a reviewer: a signature protects the components it lists, and everything else is present but unprotected.

Part B of the lab makes that measurable. A narrow approval covering tool and amount catches two of five field changes. The wide approval catches five of five. The field the narrow one misses that matters is account_id: the amount the human approved, paid to somebody else, with an approval that verifies perfectly.

So the design question is not "is the approval signed" but "which fields are inside it". Any field that changes what happens belongs inside, and the test for that is to change each field in turn and see whether the approval still verifies, which is exactly what checks 9 to 13 do.

The summary and the parameters are two different documents

A human approves by reading a sentence. A service executes by reading a structure. If those are produced independently, they can disagree, and the approval is then evidence about the sentence rather than about the action.

Part C of the lab is the cleanest version of this. The summary says "Refund 40 to T. Okafor", built from amount and account_name. The execution uses account_id. Swap the account_id and the summary is character for character identical, because the name never changed. Check 21 binds the approval to the summary and it passes. Check 22 binds it to the parameters and it fails, naming account_id.

The rule that falls out is narrow and worth stating exactly: the summary a human approves must be derived from the fields the approval covers, and no field outside those fields may change what executes. If your summary shows a display name and your call uses an id, either the id goes into the summary or the id goes into the coverage, and preferably both.

What a blanket approval is worth

Approval fatigue is real, and the usual response is a session-level approval: approve once, act many times. Part E measures one. Twelve operations, one approval, and the fields of any operation that the approval constrains: zero. Check 36 is the comparison, where a per-action approval constrains five.

Then check 37, which is the point. A payment for 40,000 slipped into that session executes under the blanket approval, because the blanket approval binds a session handle and nothing about the operation. Under a per-action approval the same call is refused, naming amount and tool.

This is not an argument that blanket approvals are always wrong. It is an argument that their value is measurable, and the measurement is the number of fields they constrain. If that number is zero, the approval is a record that somebody was present, which may be what you want, but it is not a control over what happened.

3. The control

  1. Approve content, not a reference. The approval carries a MAC over the parameters, so the executor never has to trust that the row it re-reads is the row the human saw.
  2. Cover every field that changes what happens, and test it by changing each field in turn and confirming the approval stops verifying.
  3. Derive the summary from the covered fields. If the human reads a name and the call uses an id, put the id in both.
  4. Give each approval a nonce and spend it on first use, so an approved action happens once. This is module 11's problem with a human in it.
  5. Give each approval a lifetime short enough that it expires with the context that justified it.
  6. Keep approvals per action, and where a session-level approval is genuinely needed, record how many of the operation's fields it constrains.
  7. Put the authorization in the downstream system too. OWASP: "Implement authorization in downstream systems rather than relying on LLM decisions." An approval is not a substitute for the service checking.
  8. Execute in the user's security context, which OWASP also lists, so the approval and the credential agree about who this is for.
  9. Log the approved parameters alongside the executed ones, so an incident can answer the only question that matters afterwards.

5. What it proves, and what it doesn't

Proves: that the parameters executed are the parameters a human was shown, that no covered field changed in between, that the approval was used once and within its lifetime, and that you can say which fields it covered.

Doesn't prove: that the approval was a good idea. ASI09 is precisely the case where the human read an accurate summary of a harmful action and approved it anyway, persuaded by a confident explanation. Nothing in this module helps there, and a well-bound approval of the wrong action executes exactly as reliably as a well-bound approval of the right one.

An approval that verifies can still be an approval of something else. Check 21 of the lab binds the approval to the summary the human read and then changes the destination account. The summary is identical, the approval verifies, and the money goes elsewhere. Binding is necessary and the choice of what to bind is the whole control.

6. Prove it

  • The list of actions that require approval, and what put each one on the list.
  • For one of them, the exact set of fields the approval covers.
  • A test per covered field that changes it and confirms execution is refused.
  • A test that a field outside the coverage cannot change what executes, or an explanation of why it can.
  • The code path that renders the approver's summary, showing it is built from the covered fields.
  • A test that an approval cannot be used twice.
  • The approval lifetime, and a test that an expired approval is refused.
  • Any session-wide approval, with the number of operation fields it constrains.
  • Evidence that the downstream service authorizes the call independently of the approval.
  • Log lines showing the approved parameters and the executed parameters for the same action.

Checks 2 to 4 are the whole module in three lines. The approval was genuine, the human read "Refund 40 to T. Okafor", and 4,000 went to another account. Nobody forged anything; the approval was for a proposal id and the proposal changed.

Checks 9 to 16 are the coverage question answered by measurement rather than by argument. The narrow approval catches two field changes of five, and check 16 names the one that matters: the destination account is covered by the wide approval only. If you are deciding what to put in an approval's coverage, this is the experiment to run on your own fields.

Checks 17 to 23 are the one I would put in front of a reviewer. Two parameter sets, one summary, character for character. An approval bound to that summary passes. An approval bound to the parameters names account_id. Check 23 asks whether the summary contains the field that decides where the money goes, and the answer is no, which is the defect: the human was never shown the value that mattered.

Check 30 is the sanity check that the rest rests on. An approval edited to raise the amount is refused on its MAC rather than on its content, because the covered values are inside the signed body.

Part E is the part to take to a conversation about approval fatigue. Twelve operations, one approval, zero operation fields constrained, and a 40,000 payment that was not in the session when the approval was given executing anyway. The per-action approval refuses the same call by name.

Try next:

  • Add account_id to the narrow approval's coverage and rerun. One check flips, and it is the one standing between an approved amount and an unapproved destination.
  • Change render to include account_id, then rerun check 23's experiment by hand. Decide whether showing an id to a human is an improvement or a different problem.
  • Set TTL to a day and rerun. One check flips, and the approval now outlives the shift of the person who gave it.
  • Give the blanket approval a tool field and rerun check 37. Decide how many fields it would need before you would call it an approval.
  • For a system you work on: find the approval step, and write down which fields it binds. If the answer is an identifier, you have this module's finding.

8. Check your understanding

Your approval record stores the proposal id and who approved it. What is wrong with that?

It approves a reference. The executor reads the proposal a second time, and between the human's read and that one the row can be rewritten by whatever produced it. The approval should carry a MAC over the parameters as the human saw them, so the executor can confirm it is running the same thing rather than the same identifier.

An approval covers the tool name and the amount. Is that enough?

Only if nothing else changes what happens, and the destination almost always does. The lab's narrow approval catches two field changes out of five, and the one it misses pays the approved amount to a different account. Cover every field that changes the outcome, and test coverage by changing each field and confirming the approval stops verifying.

The summary shown to the approver reads "Refund 40 to T. Okafor" and the call uses an account id. Where is the risk?

In the gap between them. The id can change while the summary stays identical, so a human approving that sentence has not seen the value that decides where the money goes. Derive the summary from the covered fields, and make sure no field outside them changes the outcome.

Is a well-bound approval a defence against prompt injection?

Partly, and only for the actions that require one. It stops an injected change to the parameters after approval, and it does nothing about a human being persuaded to approve a harmful action in the first place, which OWASP files as ASI09. It also does not replace authorization in the downstream service, which OWASP lists separately for exactly this reason.

Exercises

Change one thing and predict the output

Each exercise edits one place in labs/approvals_lab.py and asks what the run prints. The answers are the real output of each variant. One of them changes nothing, and its answer carries the measurement from a request the lab does not make.

1. The executor checks the approval against the approval

In Queue.execute, pass the approval's own stored values to check_approval in place of params. Leave the rest of the method as it is:

check_approval(self.approvals[pid],
               self.approvals[pid]["covered"], self.key,
               now=now, spent=self.spent)
What does check 5, the same rewrite against a bound approval, print?

Every option, and why
  • The check now reads the approved values, but the line that executes still formats params, and params is the second read from the store. Checking one document and running another is the defect.
  • The MAC is computed over the approval's own body, and this edit does not touch the approval. It verifies exactly as it did before.
  • Correct. Exit 1, one failure: check 5 goes from refused: changed after approval: account_id,amount to executed 4000 to ACC-999 for refund. The approval is still a real MAC and it still verifies. It is compared with itself, so it can never disagree, and the rewritten row runs. That is the same line check 2 prints for the queue with no binding at all. Check apv-01 in the Reference layer says the approval binds the parameters, which means the parameters about to execute.
  • Check 6 is an unchanged proposal. It prints executed 40 to ACC-771 for refund before and after, which is why a test of the unchanged path would not catch this edit.

2. The expiry test uses greater than

In check_approval, change if now >= approval["exp"]: to if now > approval["exp"]:.

Which check changes?

Every option, and why
  • Check 27 presents the approval 60 seconds into a 300 second lifetime. It prints executed under either comparison.
  • Correct. Exit 1, one failure: check 28 goes from refused: approval has expired to executed. Check 28 presents the approval at exactly NOW + TTL, which is the value of exp, so it sits on the one second where the two comparisons disagree. The approval is now good for 301 seconds when the lab says 300. Check apv-05 in the Reference layer asks that approvals expire, and a test at the boundary is the only kind that pins down when.
  • Check 29 prints the constant TTL, which this edit does not touch. It still prints 300, so the lab's own report of the lifetime is now one second short of the truth.
  • One second is enough here, because check 28 was written to land on the boundary.

3. The nonce is looked up but never recorded

At the end of check_approval, delete the two lines that add the nonce to spent. Leave the earlier lookup that refuses a nonce already in spent.

What does the run print differently?

Every option, and why
  • Check 24 starts with an empty record, so the lookup finds nothing and it prints executed either way.
  • Check 26 calls check_approval with no single-use record at all. It prints 2 before and after.
  • The lookup is still there, and it reads a set that nothing writes to. A check that can never find anything prints the same as no check.
  • Correct. Exit 1, one failure: check 25 goes from refused: approval has already been used to executed. The refund runs twice on one approval. Check apv-04 in the Reference layer says a nonce spent on execution, and both halves are needed: the lookup refuses a spent nonce, and the write is what spends it.

4. Only the fields the request carries are compared

In check_approval, replace the line if actual != approval["covered"]: with a comparison that walks the fields present in the request:

if any(v != approval["covered"].get(k) for k, v in actual.items()):
What does the run print differently?

Every option, and why
  • Correct. Exit 0, 52 of 52 lines identical. Every request in the lab carries every covered field, so the two comparisons agree on all of them. Measured on a request the lab does not make, an approval covering all five fields presented with account_id left out: as published the answer is refused: changed after approval: account_id, and with this edit it is executed. A covered field can now be removed where it could not be changed, and an executor that fills in a missing account from somewhere else pays that one. Check apv-02 in the Reference layer says to prove coverage by changing each field in turn. Removing a field is one of the changes.
  • The edit loosens the comparison. It cannot add account_id to a coverage list that names only amount and tool, so check 11 still prints executed.
  • The swapped request still carries account_id, with a different value. A field that is present and wrong is still caught, and check 22 still prints refused: changed after approval: account_id.
  • Checks 9 and 10 change the value of a covered field and leave it in the request, so both still refuse.

Review checklist items

These are collected into the site checklist.

  • Approvals bind the parameters, not a proposal identifier Otherwise the executor's second read of a mutable store decides what runs, and the approval is for an id that still exists.
  • Every field that changes the outcome is inside the coverage Proven by changing each field in turn and confirming the approval stops verifying. The destination is the one most often left out.
  • The approver's summary is derived from the covered fields A summary built separately can stay identical while a field outside it changes where the money goes.
  • Approvals are single use A nonce spent on execution. An approved action happens once.
  • Approvals expire Short enough that the approval does not outlive the context that justified it.
  • Downstream services authorize independently of the approval OWASP's instruction. An approval records that a human agreed, not that the caller is entitled.
  • Session-wide approvals are recorded with the fields they constrain Zero is a legitimate answer and a legitimate finding.
  • The list of actions requiring approval is written down Derived from what each action does rather than from how often it happens.
  • Approved and executed parameters are both logged So an incident can answer whether they were the same without rebuilding the state.
